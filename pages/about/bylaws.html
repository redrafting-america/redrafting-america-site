The Articles

Article I

Identity

Article I is where the organization says who it is and why it bothers. You will find its name, its mission, and the charitable purposes that give the work its shape. You will also find limits it accepts that the law does not require — no paid lobbyists, no political contributions — because a group proposing a better constitution has no standing unless it is willing to live under one first. And it names the principles used to read every page that follows, so that where the wording is unclear, the mission and the public trust decide what it means. Everything after this is machinery. This is the promise the machinery exists to keep.

Article II

Legal Structure

Article II is the ground the whole enterprise stands on. It sets out what this organization is in the eyes of the law, where it lives, and which document wins when two of them disagree. It settles who holds ultimate authority and how deliberately narrow that authority is kept, so the people doing the work answer to the mission rather than to any person, donor, or faction. You will find what leaders may see and what they must hold in confidence, the rules for any partner or spin-off entity, and how the records are kept — not for a few years, but so that future generations can still read them. Foundations are dull until they fail.

Article III

Governance Structure

Article III is about the people who will answer for all of this. It describes the seats around the table, and why some of them are deliberately held by people with nothing to gain. It sets out who may serve, and what a newcomer must finish before being trusted with a vote: study, screening, and an oath spoken aloud with a hand on these Bylaws. That last part is not theater. It is the moment an ordinary person accepts responsibility for something meant to outlast them. You will also find what leaders may be paid, who decides that, and how this Board grows from a founder working alone into a full body. A promise made out loud is harder to walk back.

Article IV

Board Nomination and Election

Article IV is about how someone earns a place at this table. It walks the path from a name put forward, through what a candidate must disclose about themselves, to the conversation with every sitting leader that happens before anyone votes. It is just as firm about what may never be a reason to choose a person: not a donation, not a famous name, not a political leaning. And it explains why staying is never automatic — everyone comes up for review, and nobody sits in the room while their own future is being discussed. A seat here is a public trust. It is lent, never given.

Article V

Leadership Structure

Article V introduces the Eleven Pillars of Leadership. Each Pillar Director both holds a Board seat and serves as a corporate officer, and the two cannot be separated — you do not get the vote without the work. The Pillars cover the whole organization: leading it, running it, recording it, funding it, keeping it lawful, telling its story, doing its research, raising its support, guarding its ethics, building its technology, and making the daily machinery work. Each Pillar carries a motto, a mission, and a plainly stated purpose describing what it protects. Authority may be delegated; responsibility for the outcome may not.

Article VI

Board Meetings

Article VI is about how decisions actually get made, and it holds to an old democratic conviction: what is done in the open can be trusted, and what is done quietly cannot. So it sets out the kinds of meeting the Board holds, the warning everyone gets, and how votes are cast — on camera, with Voting Cards raised, every choice visible to anyone who cares to watch. Meetings are open and recorded, and honest dissent is protected outright. Deciding things quietly between meetings by email, text, or private agreement is flatly prohibited. A decision nobody watched being made is not really a decision.

Article VII

Leadership Accountability

Article VII asks the question every institution eventually faces and most answer badly: what do we do when the problem is one of our own? The answer begins with fairness. Before anyone faces discipline there must be a genuine investigation, written notice of the concern, and a real chance to respond. Health is treated separately from misconduct: a leader who becomes unable to serve is assessed by independent physicians rather than by colleagues guessing, and temporary arrangements expire on their own instead of quietly becoming permanent. Suspension is a protective pause, revisited regularly — not a verdict. Nobody here sits above these pages, the founder least of all.

Article VIII

Committees

Article VIII is about sharing the work without surrendering the responsibility. Some committees are trusted to decide things. Others study, advise, and recommend, and decide nothing at all. Every one of them must say in writing which it is, what it exists for, who sits on it, and when it ends. One thing is refused outright: no small group may quietly become the real government of this organization between meetings. That is a familiar way for good institutions to hollow out, and this Article names it and shuts the door. A board that delegates its judgment has not delegated. It has resigned.

Article IX

Code of Ethics

Article IX is where this organization stops describing itself and commits itself. It opens with a single demanding idea: the truth comes first. Above comfort, above agreement, above whatever would be most convenient for us to believe. That is a hard promise to keep, and it is written down anyway, because a people asked to trust a new constitution are entitled to ask whether those proposing one live by their own words. From there the Article asks for dignity toward every person, fairness across every belief, loyalty to the mission over private advantage, and honesty about power. Being proven wrong is not failure here. It is how honest work gets done.

Article X

Conflict-of-Interest Policy

Article X is not written for crooks. It is written for the rest of us — for the ordinary moment when what is good for you and what is good for the organization stop pointing the same way. The first duty is simply to say so: out loud, in writing, early, and even when the conflict merely looks like one. From there, people with nothing at stake decide, and anyone conflicted leaves the room, physical or virtual, and stays out of it entirely. Everything goes into a register the Secretary keeps, and a summary reaches the public each year. Lending money to insiders is refused outright. Trust takes years to build and an afternoon to lose.

Article XI

Whistleblower Policy

Article XI protects the person who says the uncomfortable thing. An organization that cannot hear bad news about itself will never correct itself, so speaking up here is not merely permitted, it is expected of you. Anyone who deals with ReDrafting America may raise a concern, not only those who work here. If the worry involves the very people you would normally tell, a separate channel routes around them. You are protected for reporting honestly, not for turning out to be right. Retaliation is out of bounds, and so is quietly losing a record. Each year the organization publishes what it heard and what it did. Courage should not require luck.

Article XII

Financial Administration

Article XII is about the money, and it begins from a conviction: none of it is ours. Every dollar arrived because somebody believed in the work, which makes stewardship a moral matter before it is an accounting one. So you will find the budget, the books, the reports, and the outside examiners who look over our shoulder on a regular cycle. You will find the plain habits that keep honest people honest — no one carrying a transaction end to end, organizational funds never resting in a personal account, keys returned the day a role changes. Some things were never ours to pledge.

Article XIII

Donation Ethics

Article XIII draws a line most organizations leave comfortably blurred. Gifts are welcome; strings are not. Donors cannot buy a say in the constitutional work, a seat at the table, or immunity from being criticized by the organization they funded. Money from foreign governments and from partisan campaign committees is refused outright, though individuals who happen to be politically active may certainly give. Donor privacy is respected, but the greater the potential for influence, the greater the transparency owed to the public. Gifts may carry restrictions so long as those restrictions fit the mission. We would rather be believed than funded, if it ever came to choosing.

Article XIV

Fiduciary Duties

Article XIV is among the shortest in this document and among the ones that matter most, because it describes not a procedure but a character. Three duties are owed by everyone entrusted with authority here. Loyalty puts the organization ahead of yourself. Care means arriving prepared, asking real questions, and thinking before you decide. Obedience means keeping the organization faithful to what it said it was for. They reach past the Board to anyone who acts with its authority. Every leader here runs one part of the work, and this Article tells them plainly not to argue for it. You govern the whole, or you do not govern.

Article XV

Indemnification

Article XV is a promise the organization makes to the Directors who serve it: if your honest work draws a lawsuit, you will not have to face it alone. That promise exists for a reason that has nothing to do with lawyers and everything to do with the mission. Institutions fail when the people inside them begin protecting themselves instead of the work they came to do. So you will find who is covered, how legal costs can be met while a matter is unfolding, and who decides. And you will find where the promise stops: it shields honest service. And nothing else.

Article XVI

Digital Governance

Article XVI takes the organization's digital life seriously, because for almost everyone the website simply is the organization. Every account, domain, and credential belongs to the organization rather than to the person holding it, and access moves the moment someone's role changes. Personal information is protected and never sold, and it is not repurposed without consent or a legal obligation. If a breach occurs, affected people are told what happened, how far it reached, and what is being done about it. Published work is meant to stay reachable over time, with changes announced rather than made in the dark. A record that can be edited without a trace is not really a record.

Article XVII

Artificial Intelligence

Article XVII sets the rules for working with artificial intelligence, and it is unusually honest about it. These tools are used here, openly and often. But the line is drawn where it belongs: AI advises, humans decide. No system holds a vote, a veto, authority, or any duty of its own, and whoever relies on a tool remains responsible for checking its work. Where a formal review is called for, the organization asks several qualified independent systems chosen for real analytical diversity, each forming its own view before seeing the others, so real disagreement surfaces instead of being smoothed away. Machines can help us think. They cannot carry what only a person can carry.

Article XVIII

Constitutional Safeguards

Article XVIII is a letter to the future. It names the handful of principles this organization is built on — truth above all else, the liberty and dignity of every person, democracy that answers to the governed, independence from any political side, and a mission meant to outlast all of us — and then does something unusual with them. It makes them hard to undo. Not impossible, but hard, and never in the dark: anyone who would change them must first say plainly, in public and on the permanent record, how the change keeps faith with them. Institutions rarely lose their way all at once. They drift. This is the anchor.

Article XIX

Constitution v2.0

Article XIX is the reason ReDrafting America lives. Every generation inherits a constitution it did not write and hands the next generation whatever it had the courage to improve. Constitution v2.0 is an attempt at that improvement: a founding document written for the country we actually live in, clear enough that a citizen with no legal training can read it, honest about hard things, and built to last. Its ambition is not modest. It aims to be the World's Greatest Constitution, studied by other nations drafting their own, taking human rights as a floor rather than a ceiling. And it is offered as a proposal, never a claim to power.

Article XX

Intellectual Property

Article XX settles who owns the work — the research, the publications, the software, the branding, and the constitutional text itself — and the answer is the organization, never any individual, including work made with the help of artificial intelligence. Free to read is not the same as free to take, so you will find what you may do with the material and what needs asking first. Criticism, teaching, scholarship, and even parody remain wide open, because a document about rewriting a constitution has no business shielding itself from argument. The founder is credited as the original author: an honor, carrying no ownership and no power.

Article XXI

Emergencies

Article XXI is the plan for the days you hope never arrive. A threat to someone's safety. The systems locked or stolen. Too few leaders left to govern. A legal crisis. Money that has gone missing. A public storm. Or the need to act against someone quickly. For each, it names who may act, what they may do, and who must be told. Three rules control every crisis: emergency power is temporary, actions are reviewed afterward, and no one ever investigates themselves. History is full of institutions that never quite left the emergency. This one has decided in advance not to become one. Even at our darkest hour, the permanent principles cannot be set aside.

Article XXII

Amendments

Article XXII explains how these Bylaws change, and how the constitutional draft is revised — because a document that cannot grow will eventually be ignored, and one that can be quietly rewritten was never worth much. Any Director may propose a change, but it must be in writing, complete, and accompanied by an explanation of why it helps. Everyone receives notice and time to think, and proposals are ordinarily published so the public can watch the process unfold. Some provisions are deliberately harder to reach, and nobody may stall a proposal instead of voting on it, or take apart sideways what cannot be taken apart directly. Almost nothing here is beyond change. Nothing here may be changed in the dark.

Article XXIII

Ex Uno Floremus

Article XXIII imagines a world where this work succeeds. E Pluribus Unum answered the question of 1787: how do thirteen states become one nation? Ex Uno Floremus asks the question that comes afterward: now that we are one, what shall we become? This Article turns that question into an obligation. It prepares, long in advance, for the day Constitution v2.0 is handed over. It arranges for this organization to let go of it completely at the very moment holding on would be the easiest. What remains is a museum, an archive, a watchdog, and the brightest light this world has ever known.

Article XXIV

Dissolution

Article XXIV faces the possibility every honest institution should be willing to face: that one day it may cease to exist. Ending is treated as an extraordinary act, not an ordinary decision. It requires long advance notice, an outside legal opinion, independent review, and broad agreement. A Board too depleted to govern normally may never make that choice. Whatever assets remain go only to other charities doing compatible work, never to any individual, and gifts given for a specific purpose are honored. Two things are meant to outlive the organization: the educational work, which keeps serving the public, and the governance records, placed with libraries, archives, or museums. An institution that plans its own ending is the one you can trust while it lasts.
