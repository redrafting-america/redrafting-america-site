Founder’s Introduction

Constitution v2.0Institutional DesignCivic ParticipationProject Origins

For most of my life, I believed the United States Constitution was largely self-correcting. It was not perfect, but I trusted it. Like many Americans, I believed our institutions were strong enough to withstand almost any political challenge and that, however bitter our disagreements became, the system itself would endure.

The events of the 2010s and 2020s caused me to question that faith.

Regardless of one’s political views, those years demonstrated how much our constitutional system depends upon unwritten norms, individual restraint, and public trust.

What troubled me most was not any single election, leader, party, or political movement. It was the realization that institutions I had trusted all my life might be more fragile than I had ever imagined.

There is a particular kind of sorrow in discovering that something you believed was secure may not be secure at all.

That realization led me to ask a different question—not “How do we elect better people?” but “How do we design better institutions?”

The answer became Redrafting America.

The Institutional Weakness

As I studied our system more closely, I became increasingly concerned by structural weaknesses that had accumulated over more than two centuries. Many of our institutions still function remarkably well, but too many depend upon traditions rather than constitutional requirements. They assume future leaders will exercise restraint, political actors will place the public interest above personal gain, and unwritten norms will continue to be respected simply because they always have been.

Those assumptions may once have been sufficient.

I no longer believe they are.

But I cannot accept that fragility as inevitable.

The more closely I studied our constitutional system, the more convinced I became that honoring the Constitution does not require us to ignore its vulnerabilities. Sometimes the most faithful way to preserve an inheritance is to strengthen it before passing it on.

Why Leadership Isn’t Enough

Throughout history, societies have often responded to political crises by searching for better leaders. While leadership certainly matters, I have come to believe that lasting stability depends even more upon institutional design.

As an infrastructure engineer, I’ve learned that resilient systems cannot depend on every component performing perfectly. They must be designed to withstand failure. Constitutional government should be no different. Well-designed institutions help ordinary leaders succeed and limit the damage caused by extraordinary failures. Poorly designed institutions eventually fail, regardless of who occupies office.

Elections can change the people. Constitutional reform can improve the system itself.

That belief lies at the heart of Redrafting America.

Constitution v2.0 Is Not the Final Word

Over the coming weeks, we will begin publishing Constitution v2.0. It is not presented as a perfect document or a finished solution. It is a starting point—a comprehensive proposal intended to stimulate thoughtful discussion about what constitutional governance should look like in the twenty-first century.

Every article, every institution, and every safeguard should be questioned, debated, tested, and improved. If better ideas emerge, they should replace my own. The objective has never been to preserve my work unchanged; it has always been to create the strongest constitutional framework possible.

To help accomplish that goal, Redrafting America will include a Digital Constitutional Convention—a place where citizens can thoughtfully debate, refine, and improve the ideas presented here in the same spirit of constitutional deliberation that shaped our nation’s founding.

The Favor of Your Company Is Requested

In eighteenth-century correspondence, this phrase was simply another way of saying, “You’re invited.” I can think of no more appropriate way to conclude this introduction.

I invite you to help continue a conversation that began in Philadelphia nearly two and a half centuries ago.

If you choose to explore this project, I ask only that you do so with an open mind and a willingness to engage honestly with the ideas presented here.

You do not need to agree with every proposal. In fact, I hope you won’t. Constructive disagreement, supported by evidence and thoughtful reasoning, is one of the most valuable contributions anyone can make.

Despite everything that has caused me concern, I remain hopeful.

I still believe in the capacity of the American people to reason together, to disagree without becoming enemies, and to build something better than any one of us could design alone. I believe we can create institutions worthy of those who came before us—and strong enough to protect generations we will never meet.

My hope is that people from every political philosophy, profession, and background will help improve these ideas. If Redrafting America succeeds, Constitution v2.0 will not be remembered as the work of one individual.
